A/B Tests That Hold Up: Seven Rules for Trustworthy Experiments

Published November 2026

An A/B test is the strongest evidence in marketing and product analytics, because randomization breaks every confounding path at once. It is also the easiest kind of evidence to ruin, because every decision made after the data starts arriving is a chance to fool yourself. These are the seven rules I use to make sure a test's answer can be trusted whichever way it comes out.

1. Write the plan before the data exists

A one-page plan, dated before launch, should fix the hypothesis (what change, for whom, which direction), the randomization unit (user, account, session), one primary metric with its window, guardrail metrics that must not get worse, the minimum detectable effect, the sample size and duration in whole weeks, the decision rule, and the segments you will look at. The plan does not have to be long. It has to exist before the data does.

2. Know your minimum detectable effect

The minimum detectable effect (MDE) is the smallest true effect the test has a good chance, usually 80%, of detecting. It tells you in advance what a null result will mean. With 10,000 users per arm and a 3% baseline conversion rate, a test can only reliably detect a relative lift of roughly 20% or more. Most real changes to a landing page move conversion by a few percent, so that test is almost guaranteed to come back null. The honest summary is then "effects smaller than about 20% were undetectable", not "no effect".

Three levers shrink the MDE: more traffic or a longer test (halving the MDE takes four times the data), a metric closer to the change, and variance reduction.

3. Don't peek at the decision metric

A fixed-sample test keeps the false positive rate at 5% only if you look once, at the planned end. In simulations of A/A tests with no true difference, checking every day for four weeks and stopping at the first significant result pushes the false positive rate to close to 30%. Watch guardrails and data quality during the test, but decide at the end, or plan the interim looks in advance with a group sequential design (for example O'Brien-Fleming boundaries), which keeps the overall error near 5% while still allowing an early stop for a large effect.

4. Count your comparisons

One primary metric, three secondary metrics and five countries is up to 20 comparisons. At a 5% threshold you should expect about one false positive even if nothing works. Let one primary metric decide, declare segments in advance and correct for them (Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg), and treat unplanned findings such as "it only worked in Italy" as hypotheses for the next test.

5. Reduce variance with CUPED

CUPED uses each user's pre-experiment value of the metric to remove predictable variation from the outcome. Because that value is measured before randomization, it cannot bias the estimate; it only shrinks the variance, by a factor of 1 - ρ², where ρ is the correlation between the pre-period and in-test metric. It also corrects chance imbalances between the arms. It works best for metrics with stable individual behaviour, such as spend, sessions and transactions, and does nothing for brand-new users.

6. Run the checks that catch broken tests

  • Sample ratio mismatch. If the design splits traffic 50/50, a significant deviation means assignment or logging is broken, and the result can't be trusted whatever it shows.
  • A/A tests. Two identical arms are the cheapest way to verify that randomization, logging and analysis produce about 5% false positives.
  • Novelty effects. Plot the daily effect. If it trends, the end-of-test average does not describe the long-run effect.
  • Interference. When treated and control users interact (referrals, marketplaces, shared accounts), randomize at a higher level.
  • Long outcome windows. When the decision depends on revenue or retention, read the test as a cohort metric at a fixed horizon, such as 30, 60 or 90 days.
  • Platform split tests are not incrementality tests. Both arms are still advertising. To learn whether ads cause conversions, you need a holdout or a geo test.

7. Report the decision, the design and what you could have detected

A readout that survives scrutiny reports, in this order: the decision and how confident it is; the design (unit, split, dates, sample size, primary metric and window); the estimate with its interval; the MDE; guardrails; and any data quality issues. A non-significant result is reported as "we could not detect an effect larger than X", which is a real and useful result.

Checklist

  • Is there a written, dated plan with hypothesis, unit, primary metric, MDE, sample size, duration and decision rule?
  • Is the MDE an effect size worth acting on?
  • Does the test run in whole weeks, long enough for the outcome window?
  • Was the sample ratio checked, and has the setup passed an A/A test?
  • Is the decision read only at the planned end, or under a pre-planned sequential design?
  • Are multiple comparisons corrected or labelled as exploratory?
  • Is a null result reported with the effect size that could have been detected?
