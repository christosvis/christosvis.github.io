Practical Marketing Science

Chapter 19 Pricing research and elasticities

What to charge, which features customers will pay for, and whether a price change will make or lose money, using methods that range from a quick survey to a live price test.

19.1 A map of pricing methods

Pricing methods differ in what they ask, how close they come to real buying behavior, and how much they cost.

Method What it measures Closeness to behavior Typical use
Van Westendorp price sensitivity meter Ranges of prices customers find acceptable Low (stated perceptions) Early-stage range finding
Gabor-Granger Stated purchase intent at a set of prices Low to medium Demand curve for a single product
Choice-based conjoint Trade-offs between price and features in simulated choices Medium Packaging, plan design, willingness to pay per feature
Price tests (A/B, geo, new customers only) Real conversion and revenue at different prices High Validating a specific change
Observational elasticity from history Association between past prices and volume Medium, but confounded Context and priors only

Surveys are cheap and fast and systematically overstate willingness to buy. Live tests are accurate and constrained by fairness, regulation and customer trust. In practice, surveys narrow the options and tests decide between the final few.

19.2 Van Westendorp: finding the acceptable range

The price sensitivity meter asks four questions: at what price would the product be so cheap you would doubt its quality, cheap (a bargain), expensive but still worth considering, and too expensive to consider? Plotting the cumulative distributions of the answers gives four curves whose intersections define a range.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(19)
n = 600
reference = rng.lognormal(np.log(20), 0.35, n)          # each respondent's sense of a fair monthly price
answers = {"too cheap": reference * rng.uniform(.35, .55, n), "cheap": reference * rng.uniform(.65, .85, n),
           "expensive": reference * rng.uniform(1.15, 1.4, n), "too expensive": reference * rng.uniform(1.6, 2.2, n)}
grid = np.linspace(3, 60, 1000)
share_at_or_above = lambda x: (x[:, None] >= grid).mean(0)    # "too cheap" and "cheap" fall as price rises
share_at_or_below = lambda x: (x[:, None] <= grid).mean(0)    # "expensive" and "too expensive" rise
tc_curve, c_curve = share_at_or_above(answers["too cheap"]), share_at_or_above(answers["cheap"])
e_curve, te_curve = share_at_or_below(answers["expensive"]), share_at_or_below(answers["too expensive"])
cross = lambda a, b: grid[np.argmin(np.abs(a - b))]
points = {"Point of marginal cheapness (too cheap = expensive)": cross(tc_curve, e_curve),
          "Optimal price point (too cheap = too expensive)": cross(tc_curve, te_curve),
          "Indifference price point (cheap = expensive)": cross(c_curve, e_curve),
          "Point of marginal expensiveness (cheap = too expensive)": cross(c_curve, te_curve)}
for k, v in points.items():
    print(f"{k:58s} EUR {v:5.2f}")
## Point of marginal cheapness (too cheap = expensive)        EUR 15.32
## Optimal price point (too cheap = too expensive)            EUR 18.41
## Indifference price point (cheap = expensive)               EUR 19.38
## Point of marginal expensiveness (cheap = too expensive)    EUR 23.08
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.8))
for y, lab, ls in [(tc_curve, "Too cheap", "--"), (c_curve, "Cheap", "-"), (e_curve, "Expensive", "-"), (te_curve, "Too expensive", "--")]:
    ax.plot(grid, y, ls=ls, lw=1.8, label=lab)
lo, hi = points["Point of marginal cheapness (too cheap = expensive)"], points["Point of marginal expensiveness (cheap = too expensive)"]
ax.axvspan(lo, hi, color="#6B6B6B", alpha=0.1)
ax.set_xlim(5, 45)
ax.set_xlabel("Monthly price (EUR)")
ax.set_ylabel("Share of respondents")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, ncol=2)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Figure 19.1: Van Westendorp curves. The acceptable range runs from the point of marginal cheapness to the point of marginal expensiveness.

The method is quick and gives a sensible range, here roughly 15 to 23 EUR a month. It does not tell you how many people will actually buy at each price, and it tends to anchor on whatever respondents already pay for similar products. Use it to rule out prices, not to pick one.

Gabor-Granger goes a step further: ask whether respondents would buy at a given price, moving the price up or down depending on the answer, and build a demand curve from the responses. Multiplying demand by price (or margin) gives a revenue- or profit-maximizing price. Stated intent overstates real purchase rates, often by a large factor, so the curve’s shape is more trustworthy than its level; calibrating against a known conversion rate at the current price helps.

19.3 Choice-based conjoint: what features are worth

Conjoint analysis asks respondents to choose between product configurations that vary in price and features, several times each. Because every choice is a trade-off, the analysis can separate how much each feature adds to the appeal of a product from how much price subtracts, and so express each feature as a willingness to pay.

We simulate 400 respondents each making 10 choices among three plans, varying monthly price (9, 15, 25 or 35 EUR) and three features: invoicing tools, priority support and extra cards. The model is a conditional (multinomial) logit, where each alternative’s utility is linear in its attributes and the probability of choosing it follows the logit formula.

import warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
from statsmodels.discrete.conditional_models import ConditionalLogit

TRUE = {"price": -0.08, "invoices": 0.60, "support": 0.45, "cards": 0.30}
R, TASKS, ALTS, prices = 400, 10, 3, np.array([9, 15, 25, 35])
rows, task_id = [], 0
price_sens = rng.normal(TRUE["price"], 0.02, R)          # price sensitivity varies by respondent
for r in range(R):
    for _ in range(TASKS):
        X = np.column_stack([rng.choice(prices, ALTS), rng.integers(0, 2, (ALTS, 3))])
        utility = price_sens[r] * X[:, 0] + X[:, 1:] @ np.array([TRUE["invoices"], TRUE["support"], TRUE["cards"]])
        choice = np.argmax(utility + rng.gumbel(size=ALTS))
        rows += [[task_id, *X[a], int(a == choice)] for a in range(ALTS)]
        task_id += 1
cbc = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["task", "price", "invoices", "support", "cards", "chosen"])

fit = ConditionalLogit(cbc["chosen"], cbc[["price", "invoices", "support", "cards"]], groups=cbc["task"]).fit()
draws = rng.multivariate_normal(fit.params, fit.cov_params(), 5000)       # parameter uncertainty
wtp = -draws[:, 1:] / draws[:, [0]]
out = pd.DataFrame({"WTP (EUR/month)": wtp.mean(0), "95% low": np.quantile(wtp, .025, 0),
                    "95% high": np.quantile(wtp, .975, 0),
                    "true WTP": [-TRUE[f] / TRUE["price"] for f in ["invoices", "support", "cards"]]},
                   index=["invoices", "support", "cards"])
print(out.round(2).to_string())
##           WTP (EUR/month)  95% low  95% high  true WTP
## invoices             7.56     6.45      8.67      7.50
## support              4.14     3.10      5.20      5.62
## cards                3.18     2.13      4.24      3.75

Invoicing and extra cards are estimated close to their true values. Priority support is underestimated, and its interval misses the truth. The cause is a simplification in the model, not bad luck: it assumes every respondent shares one price sensitivity, while in the simulation (as in reality) price sensitivity varies from person to person, and it treats each respondent’s ten choices as independent observations, which makes the intervals too narrow. This is why commercial conjoint studies use hierarchical Bayes or mixed logit models, which estimate a distribution of preferences across respondents and account for repeated choices. The simple model is fine for a first look; use the hierarchical version for decisions.

With the estimated utilities you can also run a market simulator: predict the share of choices a set of plans would get, against each other and against a competitor’s offer, and test packaging options (which features go in which tier, at what prices) before building anything.

b = fit.params
def shares(plans):
    u = np.array([b["price"] * p + b["invoices"] * i + b["support"] * s + b["cards"] * c for p, i, s, c in plans])
    e = np.exp(u - u.max())
    return e / e.sum()

competitor = (15, 1, 0, 0)
for name, ours in {"Basic 9 EUR, no features": (9, 0, 0, 0),
                   "Plus 19 EUR, invoices + cards": (19, 1, 0, 1),
                   "Pro 29 EUR, all features": (29, 1, 1, 1)}.items():
    print(f"{name:32s} share vs competitor: {shares([ours, competitor])[0]:.0%}")
## Basic 9 EUR, no features         share vs competitor: 47%
## Plus 19 EUR, invoices + cards    share vs competitor: 48%
## Pro 29 EUR, all features         share vs competitor: 37%

Two cautions. Conjoint respondents make hypothetical choices with no money at stake, so absolute price sensitivity tends to be understated; trust relative feature values more than the overall price level. And the design matters: unrealistic combinations, too many attributes, or prices far outside the market range all degrade the results.

19.4 Will a price change make money?

Before any test, a simple calculation shows how much volume a price change can afford to lose (or must gain). With contribution margin \(m\) (as a share of price) and a relative price change \(\Delta p\), profit is unchanged if volume changes by

\[ \Delta q_{\text{break-even}} = \frac{-\Delta p}{m + \Delta p}. \]

rows = []
for m in [0.3, 0.6, 0.8]:
    for dp in [-0.10, +0.10]:
        dq = -dp / (m + dp)
        rows.append({"margin": f"{m:.0%}", "price change": f"{dp:+.0%}",
                     "break-even volume change": f"{dq:+.1%}", "break-even elasticity": round(dq / dp, 2)})
print(pd.DataFrame(rows).to_string(index=False))
## margin price change break-even volume change  break-even elasticity
##    30%         -10%                   +50.0%                  -5.00
##    30%         +10%                   -25.0%                  -2.50
##    60%         -10%                   +20.0%                  -2.00
##    60%         +10%                   -14.3%                  -1.43
##    80%         -10%                   +14.3%                  -1.43
##    80%         +10%                   -11.1%                  -1.11

At a 30% margin, a 10% price cut needs a 50% volume increase just to break even, which almost never happens. At an 80% margin, typical of software and many financial products, a 10% price increase breaks even if volume falls by no more than about 11%. Comparing these break-even elasticities with what you know about your customers’ sensitivity is often enough to rule a proposed change in or out.

19.5 Elasticities from data, and price tests

Observational elasticities are confounded. Regressing volume on past prices runs into the problems of Chapter 3: prices are often changed in response to demand (discounts when demand is weak, increases when it is strong), promotions coincide with campaigns, and competitors move at the same time. Log-log regressions on historical data are useful context, not decision-grade estimates.

Price tests give the real answer, with constraints that matter more than in other experiments:

  • Fairness and regulation. Showing different prices to similar customers at the same time can breach consumer protection rules and damages trust when discovered. Common alternatives are testing on new customers only, testing by market or region, testing over time with a clear before-and-after design, or testing packaging and plan structure rather than the price of an identical product.
  • Long outcome windows. A higher price may convert slightly fewer customers and retain them equally well, or convert the same number and churn more later. Read price tests on revenue or margin per acquired customer over a cohort horizon (Chapter 15), not on conversion alone.
  • Mix effects. A price change on one plan shifts customers between plans. Measure total revenue per customer across the lineup, not only the tested plan.

19.6 Checklist

  • Is the method matched to the decision: surveys for ranges and feature values, tests for final price changes?
  • Are stated-intent results treated as relative signals, calibrated where possible against real conversion?
  • Has the break-even volume change been calculated for the proposed price change at the actual margin?
  • Are historical elasticities treated as confounded context rather than estimates?
  • Does any price test respect fairness and regulatory constraints, and is it read on margin per customer over a long enough horizon, including mix effects?
