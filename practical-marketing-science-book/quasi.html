Chapter 7 Quasi-experiments: DiD, CausalImpact and synthetic control

What an intervention that was not designed as an experiment actually did: a launch in one market, a price change, a site redesign, an SEO change, an outage, a campaign limited to some regions for operational reasons.

7.1 Making the best of things that already happened

Most of the “did X work?” questions a measurement team receives are about things that already happened without a control group. You cannot randomize retroactively. What you can often do is find a comparison that plausibly shows what would have happened otherwise: other markets, other products, other customer groups, or the treated unit’s own history adjusted for what happened to everyone else.

These designs sit in the middle of the identification ladder (Chapter 3). They are much better than a raw before-and-after comparison and much weaker than a randomized test, because their key assumption, that the comparison would have moved like the treated unit, can be supported but never proven.

7.2 Difference-in-differences

Difference-in-differences (DiD) compares the change in the treated group with the change in a comparison group over the same period. Anything that moved both groups equally (seasonality, macro conditions, a national campaign) cancels out. The key assumption is parallel trends: without the treatment, the two groups would have followed parallel paths.

Parallel trends cannot be tested for the post-period, but it can be checked in the pre-period with an event study: estimate the treated-versus-control gap for each period relative to the treatment date. Before treatment, the gaps should hover around zero. After treatment, they trace out the effect over time.

7.2.1 Worked example: an event study

Thirty regions are observed for 52 weeks. In week 30, ten of them get a new local sales and marketing program. The true effect builds up gradually, reaching 8% after four weeks. Outcomes are modeled in logs so that coefficients read as approximate percentage effects.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

rng = np.random.default_rng(12)
units, weeks, start = 30, 52, 30
treated = rng.choice(units, 10, replace=False)
rows = []
unit_fe = rng.normal(np.log(500), 0.5, units)
week_fe = 0.1 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * np.arange(weeks) / 52) + 0.002 * np.arange(weeks)
for u in range(units):
    for w in range(weeks):
        rel = w - start
        effect = min(0.02 * (rel + 1), 0.08) if (u in treated and rel >= 0) else 0.0
        rows.append({"unit": u, "week": w, "treated": int(u in treated), "rel": rel,
                     "log_y": unit_fe[u] + week_fe[w] + effect + rng.normal(0, 0.03)})
df = pd.DataFrame(rows)

# Event-time dummies for treated units, binned at the ends; week -1 is the reference
df["k"] = df["rel"].clip(-10, 15)
for k in range(-10, 16):
    if k != -1:
        df[f"e{k + 10}"] = ((df["k"] == k) & (df["treated"] == 1)).astype(int)
terms = " + ".join(c for c in df.columns if c.startswith("e") and c[1:].isdigit())
fit = smf.ols(f"log_y ~ C(unit) + C(week) + {terms}", df).fit(
    cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["unit"]})

es = pd.DataFrame({"k": [int(c[1:]) - 10 for c in terms.split(" + ")],
                   "coef": [fit.params[c] for c in terms.split(" + ")],
                   "se": [fit.bse[c] for c in terms.split(" + ")]})
pre = es[es.k < -1]
print(f"Mean pre-period coefficient: {pre.coef.mean():+.3f} (max |coef| {pre.coef.abs().max():.3f})")
## Mean pre-period coefficient: -0.010 (max |coef| 0.025)
print(f"Mean effect, weeks 4+ after start: {es[es.k >= 3].coef.mean():+.3f} (true +0.080)")
## Mean effect, weeks 4+ after start: +0.074 (true +0.080)
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.9))
ax.errorbar(es.k, es.coef, yerr=1.96 * es.se, fmt="o", ms=4, capsize=2, color="#1F4E79")
ax.axhline(0, color="#6B6B6B", lw=0.8); ax.axvline(-0.5, color="#C8102E", ls="--", lw=1)
ax.set_xlabel("Weeks relative to start (endpoints binned)"); ax.set_ylabel("Effect on log outcome")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Figure 7.1: Event study: estimated treated-versus-control gap by week relative to the program start, with 95% intervals clustered by region. Flat pre-period coefficients support parallel trends.

The pre-period coefficients sit around zero, the effect ramps up after the start and settles near the true 8%. Two features of the code matter in practice: standard errors are clustered by region, because weekly observations within a region are not independent, and the reference period is the week just before treatment.

Staggered rollouts analyzed with an ordinary two-way fixed effects regression. When different units start treatment at different times and effects change over time, the standard regression compares late-treated units with already-treated units and can produce badly biased estimates, even with the wrong sign. Use estimators designed for staggered adoption (Callaway and Sant’Anna, Sun and Abraham, or the imputation approach), available in packages such as differences, diff-diff and DRDID.

7.3 CausalImpact: a model-based counterfactual

When there is a single treated unit (one country, one product, the whole website) and several unaffected time series that move with it, a structural time series model can predict the counterfactual. This is the approach behind Google’s CausalImpact package. The model is fitted on the pre-period, with the control series as regressors and a time-varying level to absorb slow drift, then projected through the post-period. The gap between actual and predicted is the effect, and the model’s forecast uncertainty gives its interval.

The assumptions are specific and worth stating in any readout:

  1. The controls were not affected by the intervention. Other countries’ sign-ups are good controls for a country-specific change; branded search volume is a bad control for a campaign, because the campaign moves it.
  2. The relationship between the treated series and the controls is stable from the pre-period into the post-period.
  3. Nothing else happened to the treated unit alone during the post-period.

7.3.1 Worked example: a site change in one market

A conversion-flow change goes live in one market on day 100 and truly lifts daily sign-ups by 10%. Three other markets, unaffected, serve as controls.

import warnings, statsmodels.api as sm
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
T, T0 = 140, 100
t = np.arange(T)
common = 100 + 0.15 * t + 8 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * t / 7)
X = np.column_stack([common * a + rng.normal(0, 2.5, T) for a in (0.8, 1.1, 0.6)])
y = common + np.cumsum(rng.normal(0, 0.4, T)) + rng.normal(0, 2.5, T)
y[T0:] *= 1.10                                    # true effect: +10% from day 100

def impact(y, X, T0, T1=None, reps=2000):
    """Fit on [0, T0), predict [T0, T1); return predicted mean, simulated paths."""
    T1 = T1 or len(y)
    res = sm.tsa.UnobservedComponents(y[:T0], level="llevel", exog=X[:T0]).fit(disp=False)
    pred = res.get_forecast(T1 - T0, exog=X[T0:T1]).predicted_mean
    sims = np.asarray(res.simulate(T1 - T0, repetitions=reps, anchor="end",
                                   exog=X[T0:T1])).reshape(T1 - T0, -1)
    return pred, sims

pred, sims = impact(y, X, T0)
cum = y[T0:].sum() - sims.sum(0)
true_cum = (y[T0:] - y[T0:] / 1.10).sum()
print(f"Cumulative effect: {y[T0:].sum() - pred.sum():.0f} sign-ups, 95% interval "
      f"[{np.quantile(cum, .025):.0f}, {np.quantile(cum, .975):.0f}]  (true {true_cum:.0f})")
## Cumulative effect: 319 sign-ups, 95% interval [149, 489]  (true 473)
print(f"Relative effect: {y[T0:].sum() / pred.sum() - 1:+.1%};  "
      f"simulated paths with effect > 0: {(cum > 0).mean():.1%}")
## Relative effect: +6.5%;  simulated paths with effect > 0: 100.0%
band = np.quantile(sims, [0.025, 0.975], axis=1)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.9))
ax.plot(t, y, lw=1.2, color="#333", label="Actual")
ax.plot(t[T0:], pred, lw=2, ls="--", label="Counterfactual")
ax.fill_between(t[T0:], band[0], band[1], alpha=0.2, label="95% interval")
ax.axvline(T0, color="#C8102E", lw=1)
ax.set_xlabel("Day"); ax.set_ylabel("Sign-ups"); ax.legend(loc="upper left")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Figure 7.2: Actual sign-ups in the treated market versus the model’s counterfactual and its 95% interval, fitted on the pre-period only.

The analysis detects the effect clearly, but the estimate (around 6 to 7%) is well below the true 10%, and the interval only just contains the truth. The treated market has its own slow drift that the controls do not explain. The model’s interval accounts for that drift on average, but any single analysis can land well off target. This is why one quasi-experimental estimate should be treated as one noisy measurement, and why placebo checks matter.

7.3.2 Placebo in time

The most useful robustness check costs one line: pretend the intervention happened earlier, inside the pre-period, and run the same analysis on data where nothing happened. A sound setup should find nothing.

pred_p, sims_p = impact(y, X, T0=70, T1=100)
cum_p = y[70:100].sum() - sims_p.sum(0)
print(f"Placebo (fake start day 70): effect {y[70:100].sum() / pred_p.sum() - 1:+.1%}, 95% interval "
      f"[{np.quantile(cum_p, .025):.0f}, {np.quantile(cum_p, .975):.0f}] sign-ups")
## Placebo (fake start day 70): effect +0.3%, 95% interval [-118, 146] sign-ups

If the placebo finds a “significant” effect, the model is not tracking the treated series well enough to trust the real result. Run several placebo dates; the share that come out significant is a direct estimate of the method’s false positive rate on your data.

Choosing control series by trying combinations until the post-period effect looks good. Pick controls on substantive grounds and on pre-period fit only, and fix them before looking at the post-period.

7.4 Synthetic control for a single unit

The synthetic control method from Chapter 6 also works for a single treated unit with a pool of untreated donors (other countries, other products). It is more transparent than a structural time series model, because the counterfactual is an explicit weighted average of named donors, and its inference comes from in-space placebos: run the method on each donor as if it had been treated and see whether the real effect stands out. With fewer than about ten donors the placebo distribution is too coarse for a meaningful p-value, and the analysis should be presented as descriptive.

7.5 When there is no control at all

Sometimes the only data is the treated series itself: an interrupted time series. You can model trend and seasonality in the pre-period and project forward, but any other change at the same time (a competitor move, a seasonal anomaly, a tracking change) is indistinguishable from the effect. Present the result as weak evidence, show the forecast interval, and look hard at the change log.

7.6 Threats and how to probe them

Threat What it looks like Probe
Concurrent shock Another change hit the treated unit in the same window Change log, placebo outcomes that should not move
Anticipation Behavior changed before the official start (announcements, pre-launch PR) Event-study leads, earlier start date
Contaminated controls The intervention also affected the comparison units Check spillover channels; drop suspect controls
Unstable relationship Controls tracked well historically but diverged for unrelated reasons Placebo-in-time tests; shorter pre-period
Selection into treatment The treated unit was chosen because it was doing badly (or well) Look for regression to the mean in the pre-period
Specification search Many models tried, one reported Pre-register controls and windows; report all variants tried

7.7 Checklist

  • Is there a plausible comparison that was not affected by the intervention?
  • For DiD: are event-study pre-period coefficients flat, and are standard errors clustered at the unit level?
  • For staggered rollouts: is a staggered-adoption estimator used instead of plain two-way fixed effects?
  • For CausalImpact: are the controls unaffected, chosen on pre-period grounds only, and fixed before looking?
  • Do placebo-in-time (and, where possible, placebo-in-space) tests come out null?
  • Does the change log show anything else hitting the treated unit in the window?
  • Does the readout name the design as quasi-experimental and list the threats that remain?
