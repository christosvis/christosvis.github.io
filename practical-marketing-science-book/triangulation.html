Chapter 14 Triangulation: deciding under contradictory evidence

What to believe about a channel when the MMM, an experiment, a platform lift study and attribution all say different things, how to turn that into a budget decision, and which test to run next.

14.1 The situation you are actually in

By this point in the book, each method has been shown to answer a specific question under specific assumptions. In practice you rarely have a single clean answer. For a typical large channel you might have an MMM posterior, one geo test from last spring, a platform lift study from a different market, and the platform’s attributed numbers updated daily. They disagree. The decision cannot wait for them to agree.

Triangulation is the discipline of combining these sources in a way that respects what each one measures, and of using the disagreement itself as a guide to what to measure next.

14.2 Every estimate has two kinds of error

When you combine estimates, it is tempting to weight them by their reported precision. That is right only if each estimate is measuring the same quantity without bias. In practice each source has two kinds of error:

  • Sampling error, the uncertainty the method reports: a confidence interval, a posterior interval.
  • Transport and bias error, which the method does not report: the gap between what it measured and the quantity you need for the decision. A geo test ran in four regions for six weeks at one spend level; the MMM coefficient may be confounded; the lift study counted pixel conversions in another market; attribution measures something else entirely.

A workable approach is to give each source an explicit allowance for the second kind of error and weight by total variance, \(1 / (\text{se}^2 + b^2)\), where \(b\) is your judgment of how far the source could be from the target quantity for reasons its own interval does not capture. The allowances are judgments, and they should be written down and argued over, just like priors (Chapter 4).

14.3 Worked example: what is search really worth?

Take the search channel from Part III. The evidence on its ROI, in euros of revenue per euro of spend:

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy import stats

ev = pd.DataFrame({
    "estimate": [7.1, 2.8, 3.4, 9.5],
    "reported se": [0.6, 0.4, 0.7, 0.3],
    "bias allowance": [3.0, 0.5, 1.0, 6.0],
    "why": ["MMM: search spend follows demand, likely confounded upward",
            "Geo test: 12 weeks, backend revenue, normal spend level",
            "Platform lift study: pixel conversions, another market",
            "Attributed ROI: not an incrementality measure"],
}, index=["MMM", "Geo test", "Lift study", "Attribution"])

def combine(est, var):
    w = 1 / var
    return (w * est).sum() / w.sum(), np.sqrt(1 / w.sum()), w / w.sum()

naive_mu, naive_sd, naive_w = combine(ev["estimate"], ev["reported se"] ** 2)
mu, sd, w = combine(ev["estimate"], ev["reported se"] ** 2 + ev["bias allowance"] ** 2)
ev["weight (naive)"], ev["weight (with allowance)"] = naive_w.round(2), w.round(2)
print(ev.drop(columns="why").to_string())
##              estimate  reported se  bias allowance  weight (naive)  weight (with allowance)
## MMM               7.1          0.6             3.0            0.13                     0.03
## Geo test          2.8          0.4             0.5            0.28                     0.75
## Lift study        3.4          0.7             1.0            0.09                     0.21
## Attribution       9.5          0.3             6.0            0.50                     0.01
print(f"\nNaive combination:            ROI {naive_mu:.2f} (sd {naive_sd:.2f})")
## 
## Naive combination:            ROI 6.75 (sd 0.21)
p_above = 1 - stats.norm.cdf(1, mu, sd)
print(f"Combination with allowances:  ROI {mu:.2f} (sd {sd:.2f}),  "
      f"P(ROI > 1) {'> 99%' if p_above > 0.99 else f'= {p_above:.0%}'}")
## Combination with allowances:  ROI 3.12 (sd 0.56),  P(ROI > 1) > 99%

The naive combination is dominated by the most precise number, attribution, which is also the one that measures the wrong thing. It lands far above every incrementality estimate. With explicit allowances, the experiments carry almost all the weight, attribution carries essentially none, and the MMM contributes a little. The combined estimate sits near the geo test, with an honest standard deviation.

The allowances do most of the work here, which is the point. A precise estimate of the wrong quantity should not dominate a noisy estimate of the right one, and the only way to stop that is to state how wrong each source could be.

14.4 From belief to decision

The decision on the table is whether to increase search spend by 20,000 EUR a month. That needs a marginal return, not the average one estimated above (Chapter 12). Search saturates quickly, so suppose the response curve implies the marginal ROI at current spend is only about 40% of the average. Then:

draws = stats.norm.rvs(mu, sd, size=100_000, random_state=1) * 0.4   # marginal ROI draws
extra = 20_000
profit = extra * (draws - 1)
print(f"Marginal ROI: median {np.median(draws):.2f}, 90% interval "
      f"[{np.quantile(draws, .05):.2f}, {np.quantile(draws, .95):.2f}]")
## Marginal ROI: median 1.25, 90% interval [0.89, 1.62]
print(f"Expected monthly profit from +{extra:,} EUR: {profit.mean():,.0f} EUR;  "
      f"P(loss) = {(profit < 0).mean():.0%};  "
      f"expected loss if wrong: {-profit[profit < 0].mean():,.0f} EUR")
## Expected monthly profit from +20,000 EUR: 5,007 EUR;  P(loss) = 13%;  expected loss if wrong: 2,227 EUR

The average ROI is comfortably above break-even, yet at the margin the picture is tighter: the expected value of the extra spend is positive, but there is a real chance it loses money. This is the gap between average and marginal returns from Chapter 1, in euros. Whether to proceed depends on how reversible the decision is and on what it would cost to reduce the uncertainty first. A spend increase that can be rolled back in a month is a cheap bet; a commitment that locks in budget for a year is not.

14.5 Diagnosing disagreement

When sources disagree, the pattern of disagreement usually points to a cause. Chapter 1 listed the common ones; with Parts II and III behind us they can be made specific.

Pattern Most likely cause What to do
MMM high, experiment lower Spend follows demand (search, retargeting) or is collinear with season Calibrate the MMM; add a demand proxy; treat MMM as upper bound
MMM low or prior-dominated, experiment positive Too little spend variation, collinearity with another channel Calibrate; create variation deliberately (flighting, geo splits)
Lift study high, geo test lower Different outcome (pixel versus backend), market, or audience Compare outcome definitions; run the geo test on the platform’s outcome too
Geo test high, lift study lower Cross-device or offline conversions the platform cannot see Feed backend conversions into the lift study
Attribution far above everything Normal: attribution counts touched, not caused, conversions Apply an incrementality factor; stop using raw attribution for budgets
Two experiments disagree Different conditions (season, spend level, creative), or noise Check MDEs; the difference may be within noise; otherwise test conditions explain it

14.6 Deciding what to test next

A measurement program has limited testing capacity: each experiment costs money (holdout revenue), time and attention. Chapter 1 suggested prioritizing by spend, uncertainty and the chance that the result changes a decision. With posteriors in hand, that can be computed.

ch = pd.DataFrame({
    "monthly spend": [90_000, 130_000, 88_000, 43_000],
    "ROI mean": [2.3, 1.8, 2.9, 1.2],
    "ROI sd": [0.15, 0.6, 1.1, 1.4],
}, index=["tv", "social", "search", "display"])
ch["P(wrong side of 1)"] = stats.norm.cdf(-np.abs(ch["ROI mean"] - 1) / ch["ROI sd"])
ch["EUR at stake"] = ch["monthly spend"] * ch["ROI sd"]
ch["priority"] = (ch["EUR at stake"] * (0.05 + ch["P(wrong side of 1)"])).round(-2)
print(ch.sort_values("priority", ascending=False).round(2).to_string())
##          monthly spend  ROI mean  ROI sd  P(wrong side of 1)  EUR at stake  priority
## display          43000       1.2    1.40                0.44       60200.0   29700.0
## social          130000       1.8    0.60                0.09       78000.0   11000.0
## search           88000       2.9    1.10                0.04       96800.0    8900.0
## tv               90000       2.3    0.15                0.00       13500.0     700.0

The score multiplies how much money the uncertainty touches by how likely the uncertainty is to flip the go/no-go call (with a floor, because even a channel safely above break-even can be misallocated at the margin). TV, measured precisely, scores lowest. Display scores high despite its small budget, because nobody knows whether it pays back at all. Social and search follow. The output is a ranked testing roadmap, and it changes every time a result comes in.

14.7 A measurement scorecard

The last tool in this part is the simplest: one row per channel, maintained over time, showing what evidence exists and how much it can be trusted.

Channel Best incrementality evidence Date Agreement across sources Confidence Next step
TV MMM, strong flighting variation Current MMM and brand search response agree High Re-test after creative change
Social Geo test, platform lift study 6 and 3 months old Within each other’s intervals Medium Heavy-up test to measure marginal return
Search Geo test 4 months old MMM far above experiment Medium (experiment) / Low (MMM) Calibrate MMM; repeat test in peak season
Display None n/a MMM posterior is mostly prior Low Go-dark geo test

The scorecard makes three things visible to everyone: which numbers rest on experiments and which on models alone, how old the evidence is, and what the team plans to do about the weakest rows. It is also the best defense against the most common failure of measurement programs, which is a single number per channel presented without any sense of how well it is known.

14.8 Checklist

  • Does each source have a stated bias allowance as well as its reported uncertainty?
  • Is attribution kept out of incrementality combinations, or given a large allowance?
  • Is the decision made on marginal returns, with probability and size of loss reported?
  • Is the reversibility of the decision considered alongside expected value?
  • Is the testing roadmap prioritized by money at stake and the chance of flipping a decision?
  • Is there a channel scorecard with evidence type, age and confidence, kept up to date?
