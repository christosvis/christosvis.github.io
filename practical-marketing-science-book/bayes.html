Chapter 4 Bayesian reasoning in practice

How to express what you know about a marketing effect as a probability, how to combine evidence from different sources, and how to turn uncertainty into a decision instead of a significance verdict.

4.1 Why Bayesian thinking fits marketing

This chapter is not an argument that every analysis should be Bayesian. Frequentist methods are fine, and many results in later chapters are reported with ordinary confidence intervals. It is an argument that the way of thinking fits marketing decisions unusually well, for four reasons.

  1. Stakeholders ask Bayesian questions. “How likely is it that this channel pays for itself?” is a question about the probability of a hypothesis given the data. A p-value answers a different question (the probability of data this extreme if the effect were zero), and the mismatch causes endless confusion.
  2. Prior knowledge exists and should be used. You usually know something before the data arrives: past experiments, industry benchmarks, the physical impossibility of a channel driving 300% of revenue. Ignoring it is not neutral; it is a choice to pretend you know nothing.
  3. Data is often small and noisy. Weekly data for a two-year MMM has about 100 observations and a dozen or more parameters. Priors are what keep such models from producing absurd answers.
  4. Evidence accumulates. A measurement program produces a stream of experiments and model refreshes. Bayesian updating gives a principled way to combine them instead of treating each one in isolation.

The mechanics are compact. Start with a prior distribution over the quantity of interest. Combine it with the likelihood of the observed data. The result is the posterior, which is what you now believe. Every summary you report (an estimate, an interval, a probability that the effect exceeds a threshold) is read off the posterior.

4.2 Worked example: an A/B test, read two ways

A landing page test runs with 12,000 users per arm. The control converts 360 users (3.00%) and the variant converts 402 (3.35%). The frequentist reading:

import numpy as np
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest

conv = np.array([402, 360]); n = np.array([12_000, 12_000])
z, p = proportions_ztest(conv, n, alternative="two-sided")
rel_lift = (conv[0] / n[0]) / (conv[1] / n[1]) - 1
print(f"Observed relative lift: {rel_lift:+.1%}, two-sided p = {p:.3f}")
## Observed relative lift: +11.7%, two-sided p = 0.122

The result is “not significant at 5%”, and in many organizations the conversation ends there, with the variant discarded. The Bayesian reading starts from a weak prior (Beta(1, 1), uniform over conversion rates) and draws from the posterior of each arm:

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
draws = 200_000
post_a = rng.beta(1 + 360, 1 + 12_000 - 360, draws)   # control
post_b = rng.beta(1 + 402, 1 + 12_000 - 402, draws)   # variant
lift = post_b / post_a - 1

print(f"P(variant better than control) = {(post_b > post_a).mean():.1%}")
## P(variant better than control) = 93.9%
print(f"Relative lift: median {np.median(lift):+.1%}, 95% interval "
      f"[{np.quantile(lift, .025):+.1%}, {np.quantile(lift, .975):+.1%}]")
## Relative lift: median +11.6%, 95% interval [-3.0%, +28.5%]
# Expected loss: how much conversion rate we give up, on average, if we pick the wrong arm
loss_ship_b = np.maximum(post_a - post_b, 0).mean()
loss_keep_a = np.maximum(post_b - post_a, 0).mean()
print(f"Expected loss if we ship the variant: {loss_ship_b*100:.3f} pp")
## Expected loss if we ship the variant: 0.006 pp
print(f"Expected loss if we keep the control: {loss_keep_a*100:.3f} pp")
## Expected loss if we keep the control: 0.356 pp

The data are identical; what changes is what you report. “There is roughly a 94% chance the variant is better, the likely lift is around 12% but could plausibly be anywhere from slightly negative to nearly 30%, and the expected cost of shipping it if we are wrong is under a hundredth of a percentage point of conversion, while the expected cost of keeping the control is over a third of a point” is a statement a product owner can act on. It also makes the real trade-off visible: if the variant is cheap to ship and easy to revert, a 94% probability with a small expected loss is a good bet; if shipping it is costly or irreversible, it may be worth running longer.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, with a weak prior and a reasonable sample size, the Bayesian interval is numerically close to the frequentist one. The Bayesian framing does not create evidence that is not there. Second, the decision rule must be fixed before the test, exactly as with significance testing. “Ship if the probability of being better exceeds 95% and expected loss is below X” is a rule; looking at the posterior every day and stopping when it looks good is peeking, and it inflates false positives in Bayesian and frequentist analysis alike (Chapter 5).

4.3 Worked example: combining a model and an experiment

A marketing mix model estimates the incremental return on ad spend (iROAS) of a paid social channel at 1.8, with posterior standard deviation 0.6. A geo experiment on the same channel then estimates 1.1, with standard error 0.3. Which do you believe?

Neither on its own. If both estimates are roughly normal and measure the same quantity, the combined estimate weights each by its precision (the inverse of its variance):

\[ \mu_{\text{post}} = \frac{\mu_1/\sigma_1^2 + \mu_2/\sigma_2^2}{1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2}, \qquad \sigma_{\text{post}} = \left(\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\right)^{-1/2} \]

from scipy import stats

mmm = (1.8, 0.6); geo = (1.1, 0.3)
prec = [1/mmm[1]**2, 1/geo[1]**2]
mu = (mmm[0]*prec[0] + geo[0]*prec[1]) / sum(prec)
sd = sum(prec) ** -0.5

for name, (m, s) in {"MMM alone": mmm, "Geo test alone": geo, "Combined": (mu, sd)}.items():
    p_win = 1 - stats.norm.cdf(1, m, s)
    print(f"{name:15s} iROAS {m:.2f} (sd {s:.2f})   P(iROAS > 1) = {p_win:.0%}")
## MMM alone       iROAS 1.80 (sd 0.60)   P(iROAS > 1) = 91%
## Geo test alone  iROAS 1.10 (sd 0.30)   P(iROAS > 1) = 63%
## Combined        iROAS 1.24 (sd 0.27)   P(iROAS > 1) = 81%
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
x = np.linspace(-0.5, 4, 600)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.9))
curves = [(mmm, "MMM", "--"), (geo, "Geo test", ":"), ((mu, sd), "Combined", "-")]
for (m, s), lab, ls in curves:
    ax.plot(x, stats.norm.pdf(x, m, s), ls=ls, lw=2, label=f"{lab}: {m:.2f}")
ax.axvline(1, color="#6B6B6B", lw=0.8)
ax.text(1.03, ax.get_ylim()[1] * 0.9, "break-even", fontsize=8)
ax.set_xlabel("iROAS")
ax.set_yticks([])
ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Figure 4.1: Precision-weighted combination of a model estimate and an experiment. The experiment dominates because it is more precise.

The combined estimate sits much closer to the experiment because the experiment is four times as precise. This is the core logic of calibrating an MMM with experiments (Chapter 11): the experiment becomes a prior, or a likelihood term, that pulls the model’s channel estimate toward what was measured directly.

The formula assumes both numbers measure the same thing. In practice they often do not: the experiment ran in two markets for four weeks at a particular spend level, while the MMM coefficient is an average over two years and all markets. Before combining, check that the spend level, time period and outcome definition match closely enough. If they do not, the honest move is to model the difference (for example, by widening the experiment’s uncertainty to reflect the gap) rather than pretending it is not there.

4.4 Choosing priors

Priors attract suspicion because they look subjective. In marketing models they are usually the opposite: they encode hard constraints everyone agrees on and stop the model from wandering into impossible territory. Three kinds are useful.

  • Weakly informative priors rule out the absurd without favoring any particular plausible value. Example: a channel’s ROI is positive and very unlikely to exceed 20.
  • Informative priors from evidence, such as a past experiment or a meta-analysis of similar channels. These should be documented with their source.
  • Structural priors on things like how quickly an ad effect decays (adstock) or how fast returns saturate, where the data alone rarely pins down the answer.

A useful habit is the prior predictive check: before seeing the data, simulate what the prior implies about observable quantities and ask whether that looks sane. Suppose a business spends 50,000 per week on media and makes 100,000 per week in revenue, and you are choosing a prior for the media coefficient.

import pandas as pd
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
spend, revenue = 50_000, 100_000
priors = {
    "Normal(0, 10) on coefficient": rng.normal(0, 10, 100_000),
    "LogNormal(log 1, 0.5) on ROI": rng.lognormal(np.log(1.0), 0.5, 100_000),
}
rows = {}
for name, beta in priors.items():
    share = beta * spend / revenue              # implied share of revenue driven by media
    rows[name] = {"5%": np.quantile(share, .05), "median": np.median(share),
                  "95%": np.quantile(share, .95),
                  "P(impossible)": ((share < 0) | (share > 1)).mean()}
pct = (pd.DataFrame(rows).T * 100).round(0).astype(int).astype(str) + "%"
print(pct.to_string())   # P(impossible): share below 0% or above 100%
##                                  5% median   95% P(impossible)
## Normal(0, 10) on coefficient  -824%     1%  823%           92%
## LogNormal(log 1, 0.5) on ROI    22%    50%  114%            8%

The “uninformative” Normal(0, 10) prior says it is entirely plausible that media drives minus 800% or plus 800% of revenue, and it puts over 90% of its mass on impossible values. It is not neutral; it is wrong. The lognormal prior on ROI keeps media’s share between roughly 20% and 110% of revenue, which is still generous but at least physically possible, and it encodes the belief that advertising does not destroy revenue.

The second habit is sensitivity analysis: refit with a meaningfully different reasonable prior and see whether the decision changes. If it does, the data are not strong enough to settle the question, and the honest conclusion is that you need more data or an experiment, not that you should pick the prior that gives the preferred answer.

Choosing priors after seeing which answer they produce. Priors must be set and documented before the model is fitted, with a source for each informative one. Otherwise the prior is just a dial for producing the result someone wanted.

4.5 From uncertainty to decisions

The point of a posterior is to make decisions, and a decision needs two ingredients a significance test does not provide: the size of the possible gain and loss, and how much each outcome matters.

Consider a budget decision on the paid social channel from the example above, where break-even is an iROAS of 1. The combined posterior says the channel probably pays back, but not certainly. The expected value of an extra 100,000 in spend is

\[ E[\text{profit}] = 100{,}000 \times (E[\text{iROAS}] - 1), \]

and you can also compute the probability of losing money and the expected size of that loss. A useful habit is to report all three: expected gain, probability of loss, and expected loss if wrong. Two channels with the same 80% probability of being profitable can be very different bets if one has a long left tail.

Bayesian decision analysis also gives a clean answer to “should we test this?”: run the experiment if the expected reduction in bad-decision losses exceeds the cost of the test (including the revenue lost in the holdout). This is the value of information idea from Chapter 1, made quantitative.

4.6 Checklist

  • Are priors set and documented before fitting, with a source for each informative prior?
  • Has a prior predictive check confirmed that the priors imply plausible outcomes?
  • Has a sensitivity analysis shown whether a different reasonable prior changes the decision?
  • When combining a model and an experiment, do they measure the same quantity (spend level, period, market, outcome)?
  • Was the decision rule (thresholds on probability and expected loss) fixed before looking at results?
  • Does the readout report probability of loss and expected loss, not just a point estimate?
