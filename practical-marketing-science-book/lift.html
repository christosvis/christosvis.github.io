Chapter 8 Reading platform lift studies

How much to trust an ad platform’s own incrementality study, what exactly it measured, and how to turn its result into numbers you can use for budgeting and for correcting the platform’s everyday reporting.

8.1 What a lift study is

The large ad platforms offer incrementality studies on their own inventory: conversion lift studies that hold out a random share of the target audience from seeing your ads, geo-based variants that hold out regions, and brand lift studies that survey exposed and unexposed users. The details and names change often, so check your platform’s current documentation, but the logic is stable.

User-level conversion lift is a real randomized experiment. The platform splits the eligible audience at random into a test group, which can be shown your ads, and a holdout, which is not (holdout users see other advertisers’ ads instead). Conversions in the two groups are compared. Because assignment is random, the comparison is causal in the same sense as any A/B test.

That makes a platform lift study much stronger evidence than the platform’s attribution reporting, which measures who converted after an ad, not who converted because of one (Chapter 1). It does not make it a complete answer. The randomization is sound; the questions are about what outcome was measured, on whom, under which conditions.

8.2 What exactly was measured

Read every lift study against these questions before using its result.

Question Why it matters What to look for
What is the outcome? The platform can only count conversions it observes Pixel / SDK events versus conversions uploaded from your backend
Who was randomized? Results apply to the audience in the test, not to everyone Targeting, exclusions, campaigns included
How is the effect scaled? Intent-to-treat versus effect on users who actually saw ads “Reach” or “exposed” share; whether results are per eligible or per reached user
How big was the holdout? Holdout size drives precision Holdout share, number of conversions in the holdout
Which interval is reported? Not every platform reports 95% intervals Confidence or credibility level; Bayesian probabilities of lift
Was delivery normal? Campaign changes during the test change what was measured Budget, bidding, creative changes in the test window
What window? Long-lag outcomes may fall outside the study Study duration, post-period, conversion window

The single most important item is the first. If the study counts only pixel-tracked conversions, it inherits all the signal loss of tracking (Chapter 2): users who convert on another device or without consent are missing from both groups. That usually understates the absolute incremental count while leaving the relative lift roughly intact. Wherever the platform allows it, feed system-of-record conversions into the study (server-side events or offline conversion uploads), so the study measures the outcome you actually care about.

8.3 Worked example: from a study readout to usable numbers

A study ran for four weeks with a 10% holdout. The platform reports these totals for the period:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

N_t, N_c = 2_000_000, 200_000      # users in test and holdout groups (10% holdout)
conv_t, conv_c = 6_300, 560        # conversions observed in each group
spend = 150_000                    # media spend during the study (EUR)
attributed = 2_100                 # conversions the platform attributed to the campaign

p_t, p_c = conv_t / N_t, conv_c / N_c
incr = (p_t - p_c) * N_t                                       # incremental conversions
se = np.sqrt(p_t * (1 - p_t) / N_t + p_c * (1 - p_c) / N_c) * N_t
lo, hi = incr - 1.96 * se, incr + 1.96 * se

out = pd.DataFrame({
    "estimate": [p_t / p_c - 1, incr, spend / incr, incr / attributed],
    "95% low":  [lo / (p_c * N_t), lo, spend / hi, lo / attributed],
    "95% high": [hi / (p_c * N_t), hi, spend / lo, hi / attributed],
}, index=["Relative lift", "Incremental conversions", "Incremental CPA (EUR)", "Incrementality factor"])
print(out.round(2).to_string())
##                          estimate  95% low  95% high
## Relative lift                0.12     0.04      0.21
## Incremental conversions    700.00   211.48   1188.52
## Incremental CPA (EUR)      214.29   126.21    709.29
## Incrementality factor        0.33     0.10      0.57
print(f"\nPlatform-attributed CPA: {spend / attributed:.0f} EUR")
## 
## Platform-attributed CPA: 71 EUR

Three things stand out.

The relative lift is modest and the interval is wide. A 12.5% lift with an interval from roughly 4% to 21% is typical. The precision is limited by the holdout: only 560 conversions in the control group.

The incremental CPA is several times the attributed CPA. The platform’s reporting says each conversion costs about 71 EUR. The study says each incremental conversion costs around 214 EUR, and plausibly anywhere from about 125 to over 700. Note that the interval on a cost ratio is skewed: it is computed by inverting the interval on incremental conversions, not by adding and subtracting a margin.

The incrementality factor connects the two. About a third of attributed conversions are incremental here, with a wide range. This factor is how a lift study gets used day to day (below).

8.3.1 Intent-to-treat versus reached users

The calculation above compares everyone randomized to the test group with everyone in the holdout, including test-group users who never actually saw an ad. That is the intent-to-treat effect, and it is what matters for budgeting, because it answers “what did running this campaign do?”.

Some studies instead report the effect per reached user, comparing exposed users with holdout users who would have been exposed. If only 45% of the test group was reached, and unreached users were unaffected, the effect per reached user is the intent-to-treat effect divided by 0.45. Both numbers are legitimate. Mixing them up overstates or understates the effect by a factor of two or more, so check which one the report gives before comparing studies.

8.4 Sizing the holdout

Holdout size trades precision against cost: holdout users are not advertised to, so a bigger holdout means more conversions forgone during the study. The table shows the minimum detectable relative lift (80% power, 5% two-sided) for the audience and baseline rate above, by holdout share.

from scipy import stats
N, p = 2_200_000, 0.0028
z = stats.norm.ppf(0.975) + stats.norm.ppf(0.8)
rows = []
for h in [0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.50]:
    n_c, n_t = N * h, N * (1 - h)
    mde = z * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) * (1 / n_t + 1 / n_c)) / p
    rows.append({"holdout share": f"{h:.0%}", "holdout conversions": round(n_c * p),
                 "MDE (relative lift)": f"{mde:.1%}"})
print(pd.DataFrame(rows).to_string(index=False))
## holdout share  holdout conversions MDE (relative lift)
##            5%                  308               16.4%
##           10%                  616               11.9%
##           20%                 1232                8.9%
##           30%                 1848                7.8%
##           50%                 3080                7.1%

Going from a 10% to a 20% holdout narrows the MDE noticeably; beyond about 30%, the gains are small and the cost keeps growing. If the expected lift is around 10%, a 10% holdout on this audience is marginal: there is a real chance the study comes back “inconclusive”. Size the study before launching it, the same way as any test (Chapter 5).

8.5 Using a lift study

Incrementality factors. The most practical use is to calibrate everyday platform reporting. Divide incremental conversions by attributed conversions in the same study and apply that factor to the platform’s attributed numbers going forward. Platform CPA of 71 EUR and a factor of 0.33 imply an incremental CPA of about 214 EUR, which is the number to compare with other channels and with the value of a conversion. Factors are specific to a platform, campaign type, market and attribution setting, and they drift as targeting and tracking change. Refresh them at least a few times a year and whenever the setup changes materially.

MMM calibration. A lift study is also an input for a marketing mix model, as a prior or a calibration target for that channel (Chapter 11), with the caveats above about outcome definition and scaling.

Cross-checks. Because the platform designs, runs and reports the study, an occasional independent check with a geo test on backend outcomes (Chapter 6) is good practice for the largest channels. The point is not that platforms report dishonestly; it is that their study measures their tracked outcome under their conditions, and you want to know how well that transfers to your business outcome.

Extending a lift result to another market, campaign type or spend level without saying so. A study on prospecting campaigns in one country, at one budget, says little about retargeting in another country at double the budget. When a result is used outside the conditions it was measured in, write that down as an assumption.

8.6 Brand lift studies

Brand lift studies survey users in exposed and holdout groups about ad recall, awareness, consideration or intent. They measure stated attitudes, not behavior, and typically have small response samples, so their intervals are wide. They are useful for comparing creative or formats on upper-funnel goals, and as a sanity check that a brand campaign registers at all. They are not evidence of incremental conversions or revenue; for that, use conversion lift or geo tests on business outcomes.

8.7 Checklist

  • Is the outcome the platform’s tracked conversion or your system-of-record conversion?
  • Is the result intent-to-treat or per reached user, and does any comparison mix the two?
  • How many conversions were in the holdout, and what MDE did the design have?
  • Which confidence or credibility level is reported?
  • Did budget, bidding or creative change during the study?
  • Is the incrementality factor computed with its interval and stored with the conditions it was measured under (platform, campaign type, market, attribution setting, date)?
  • If the result is applied to another market, campaign or spend level, is that stated as an assumption?
