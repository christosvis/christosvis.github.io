Chapter 15 Cohorts, retention, LTV and payback

How much an acquired customer is worth, how long it takes to earn back what it cost to acquire, and therefore how much each channel can afford to pay per customer. These numbers turn acquisition measurement from “cheapest conversions” into “most valuable conversions”.

15.1 Why cohorts

Blended metrics such as “monthly churn” or “average revenue per customer” mix customers acquired at different times, through different channels, at different ages. A business acquiring fast will show low blended churn simply because most of its customers are new, and new customers have not had time to leave. A business whose acquisition is slowing will show rising churn with no change in customer behavior at all.

A cohort is a group of customers acquired in the same period (and, for marketing purposes, usually through the same channel). Tracking each cohort’s activity by months since acquisition separates the effect of customer age from the effect of calendar time. The standard view is the cohort triangle: one row per cohort, one column per month since acquisition, with the most recent cohorts having the fewest observed months.

15.2 Worked example: two channels, twelve cohorts

We simulate twelve monthly cohorts of 800 customers from each of two channels. Each customer has their own monthly probability of leaving, drawn from a beta distribution; the channels differ in that distribution.

  • Search brings customers with moderate, fairly uniform churn: Beta(1.2, 5.0), mean about 19% a month.
  • Social brings a more mixed group: many who leave quickly and a loyal core. Beta(0.7, 1.6), mean about 30% a month.

Each active customer contributes 25 EUR of margin per month. We observe each cohort up to the present, so the oldest cohort has twelve months of history and the newest one month.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy.special import betaln
from scipy.optimize import minimize

rng = np.random.default_rng(15)
CH = {"search": (1.2, 5.0), "social": (0.7, 1.6)}     # Beta(a, b) of monthly churn probability
N_COHORTS, SIZE, MARGIN, HORIZON = 12, 800, 25.0, 36

def survival(t, a, b):
    """sBG survival: P(still active at month t), t = 0, 1, 2, ..."""
    return np.exp(betaln(a, b + t) - betaln(a, b))

rows = []
for c, (a, b) in CH.items():
    for k in range(N_COHORTS):
        theta = rng.beta(a, b, SIZE)                 # each customer's churn probability
        months_active = rng.geometric(theta)         # months until they leave (>= 1)
        for m in range(N_COHORTS - k):               # months observed so far for this cohort
            rows.append({"channel": c, "cohort": k, "month": m, "active": (months_active > m).sum()})
cohorts = pd.DataFrame(rows)

tri = cohorts.query("channel == 'social'").pivot(index="cohort", columns="month", values="active")
print("Social cohorts, share still active by month since acquisition:")
## Social cohorts, share still active by month since acquisition:
print(tri.div(tri[0], axis=0).iloc[:, :9].round(2).to_string(na_rep=""))

15.3 Heterogeneity makes churn fall over time

Look along any row of the triangle: the share of customers leaving each month falls as the cohort ages. Nobody’s behavior is changing. Each customer has a fixed churn probability, but the customers most likely to leave do so first, so the survivors are increasingly the loyal ones. Any retention model that assumes a constant churn rate will get this wrong, and in a predictable direction.

The shifted-beta-geometric (sBG) model captures exactly this: each customer has a constant churn probability, and those probabilities vary across customers according to a beta distribution. It has two parameters and fits cohort data by maximum likelihood, with right-censoring handled naturally (customers still active at the last observed month contribute what we know about them: that they survived at least that long).

def cohort_arrays(c):
    d = cohorts[cohorts.channel == c]
    return [g.sort_values("month")["active"].to_numpy() for _, g in d.groupby("cohort")]

def fit_sbg(arrays):
    def nll(log_ab):
        a, b = np.exp(log_ab)
        ll = 0.0
        for n in arrays:
            t = np.arange(1, len(n))
            p_churn = np.exp(betaln(a + 1, b + t - 1) - betaln(a, b))   # P(leave in month t)
            ll += ((n[:-1] - n[1:]) * np.log(p_churn)).sum() + n[-1] * np.log(survival(len(n) - 1, a, b))
        return -ll
    return np.exp(minimize(nll, [0.0, 0.0], method="Nelder-Mead").x)

t = np.arange(HORIZON)
res = {}
for c, (a_true, b_true) in CH.items():
    arrays = cohort_arrays(c)
    a, b = fit_sbg(arrays)
    # Naive alternative: constant churn estimated from the first three months of retention
    first3 = np.mean([n[m + 1] / n[m] for n in arrays for m in range(min(3, len(n) - 1))])
    # Bootstrap over cohorts for an interval on the sBG-based LTV
    boot = [MARGIN * survival(t, *fit_sbg([arrays[i] for i in rng.integers(0, len(arrays), len(arrays))])).sum()
            for _ in range(100)]
    res[c] = {"fitted a, b": f"{a:.2f}, {b:.2f}", "true a, b": f"{a_true}, {b_true}",
              "LTV true": MARGIN * survival(t, a_true, b_true).sum(),
              "LTV sBG": MARGIN * survival(t, a, b).sum(),
              "sBG 90% low": np.quantile(boot, 0.05), "sBG 90% high": np.quantile(boot, 0.95),
              "LTV constant churn": MARGIN * (first3 ** t).sum()}
ltv = pd.DataFrame(res).T
num = ["LTV true", "LTV sBG", "sBG 90% low", "sBG 90% high", "LTV constant churn"]
ltv[num] = ltv[num].astype(float).round(0).astype(int)
print(f"36-month margin per acquired customer (EUR):\n{ltv.to_string()}")
## 36-month margin per acquired customer (EUR):
##        fitted a, b true a, b  LTV true  LTV sBG  sBG 90% low  sBG 90% high  LTV constant churn
## search  1.13, 4.66  1.2, 5.0       229      235          228           241                 147
## social  0.76, 1.79  0.7, 1.6       213      205          199           213                 107

The sBG model recovers both channels’ true lifetime value within a few percent from at most twelve months of data, and its interval covers the truth. The constant-churn extrapolation, the kind of calculation many dashboards do, underestimates lifetime value by a third to a half, and it underestimates social, the more heterogeneous channel, the most.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 2.9))
for c, color in zip(CH, ["#1F4E79", "#C8102E"]):
    arrays = cohort_arrays(c)
    obs = [np.sum([n[m] for n in arrays if len(n) > m]) / np.sum([n[0] for n in arrays if len(n) > m])
           for m in range(N_COHORTS)]
    a, b = fit_sbg(arrays)
    first3 = np.mean([n[m + 1] / n[m] for n in arrays for m in range(min(3, len(n) - 1))])
    ax.plot(range(N_COHORTS), obs, "o", ms=4, color=color)
    ax.plot(t, survival(t, a, b), color=color, lw=2, label=f"{c}: sBG")
    ax.plot(t, first3 ** t, color=color, ls="--", lw=1.2, label=f"{c}: constant churn")
ax.axvline(N_COHORTS - 0.5, color="#6B6B6B", lw=0.8)
ax.set_xlabel("Months since acquisition")
ax.set_ylabel("Share still active")
ax.legend(fontsize=8, ncol=2)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

15.4 Payback and what a channel can afford

Lifetime value only matters relative to acquisition cost. Two numbers turn it into a decision:

  • Payback period: months until cumulative margin per acquired customer exceeds the cost of acquiring them. It governs cash: a business that has to fund acquisition from its balance sheet cares how long the money is out.
  • Maximum affordable CAC: the lifetime value (over a horizon the business is willing to count, discounted if the horizon is long) minus any required margin of safety. It is the ceiling for bids and channel targets.
cac = {"search": 180, "social": 140}
out = []
for c in CH:
    a, b = fit_sbg(cohort_arrays(c))
    cum = MARGIN * np.cumsum(survival(t, a, b))
    out.append({"channel": c, "CAC": cac[c], "12-month value": cum[11], "36-month LTV": cum[-1],
                "payback (months)": int(np.argmax(cum >= cac[c])) + 1 if cum[-1] >= cac[c] else None,
                "LTV / CAC": cum[-1] / cac[c]})
print(pd.DataFrame(out).set_index("channel").round(1).to_string())
##          CAC  12-month value  36-month LTV  payback (months)  LTV / CAC
## channel                                                                
## search   180           147.9         234.6                19        1.3
## social   140           124.9         205.1                16        1.5

Social’s customers leave faster in the first months, which a retention chart alone would hold against it. But they are cheaper to acquire and their loyal core keeps paying, so social pays back sooner and returns more per euro than search. Retention curves and acquisition costs have to be read together.

Both channels are thin, though: each euro of acquisition cost returns only 1.3 to 1.5 euros of margin over three years, and a third or more of that is projection beyond what has been observed. These are also attributed acquisition costs. For budgeting, the CAC should be the incremental one from Parts II and III, which is usually higher, and at that cost neither channel may pay back within the horizon. Whether to keep acquiring at these levels depends on the business’s cash position and how much it trusts the long tail of the projection.

Judging acquisition channels on cost per customer while their customers have very different value. A channel with twice the CAC can be the better buy if its customers stay twice as long. Wherever possible, set channel targets on value per euro, not customers per euro.

15.5 Practical choices

Margin, not revenue. Lifetime value should be built from contribution margin per period (revenue minus variable costs such as payment processing, support and incentives), or it overstates what a customer is worth.

Discounting and horizon. A euro earned in year three is worth less than one earned now, and projections that far out are uncertain. Most teams cap the horizon (24 or 36 months) and discount at the business’s cost of capital.

Definition of “active”. For subscription businesses, active means paying. For businesses where customers can lapse and return (most transaction-based products, including many fintech products where an account stays open but goes unused), a contractual churn model like sBG does not fit; non-contractual models such as BG/NBD and Pareto/NBD model purchase frequency and the probability that a customer is still “alive” from transaction histories. PyMC-Marketing includes Bayesian versions of these.

Early prediction. Waiting a year to learn a cohort’s value is too slow for marketing decisions. Two approaches help: fit a retention model to the first few months (as above, the model extrapolates far better than averages), and predict value from early behavior (activation steps, first transactions) with a model trained on older cohorts.

Measuring value effects of marketing. When an experiment changes who is acquired (an incentive, a new channel), its effect on value must be read at a horizon long enough for value to show: a fixed cohort horizon such as 60 or 90 days after acquisition, not two weeks.

Treating an LTV/CAC ratio of 3 as a universal target. The right ratio depends on margin, payback speed, cost of capital and how much of the LTV is projection rather than observation. Use the ratio as a summary, and make decisions on payback and marginal returns.

15.6 Checklist

  • Are retention and value analyzed by cohort (acquisition month and channel), not as blended averages?
  • Does the retention model allow churn to fall as cohorts age, rather than assuming a constant rate?
  • Is lifetime value built from margin, over a stated horizon, with discounting where the horizon is long?
  • Is the uncertainty of projected LTV reported, and how much of it is projection beyond observed months?
  • Are channel CAC targets set on value, and on incremental rather than attributed acquisition cost?
  • For experiments that change customer mix, is value read at a long enough horizon?
