Practical Marketing Science

Chapter 2 Data and signal foundations

Whether your data can support the analysis you are about to run. Most failed measurement projects fail here, quietly, long before any model is fitted.

2.1 The measurement dataset

Almost every method in this book (MMM, geo tests, quasi-experiments, channel reporting) consumes the same underlying object: a panel of spend, delivery and outcomes by date, market and channel. Building that panel once, cleanly, and reusing it is the highest-leverage data work a measurement team can do.

Field group Examples Notes
Keys date, market (country, region, DMA), channel, sub-channel, campaign Fix the grain first. Daily by market by channel covers most needs.
Spend media cost in a single currency, fees Agree whether agency and tech fees are included, and keep it constant.
Delivery impressions, clicks, reach, GRPs Needed when spend and delivery decouple (CPM changes, auction shifts).
Outcomes (system of record) sign-ups, accounts opened, first transactions, revenue From the backend or warehouse, not from pixels.
Outcomes (tracked) platform conversions, analytics conversions Kept separately; used for diagnostics, never as the target.
Controls price, promotions, product launches, holidays, competitor activity, macro indicators Anything else that moves the outcome.
Events tracking changes, naming changes, outages, policy changes See the change log below.

Two rules save a lot of pain. First, keep tracked and system-of-record outcomes in separate columns and never let one silently replace the other. Second, store the raw platform extracts and build the panel from them with versioned code, so that a mapping change can be replayed over history instead of only applying going forward.

2.2 Naming and taxonomy

Channel-level analysis is only as good as the mapping from campaign to channel. A campaign called DE_Search_Brand_Q3 is easy; one called test_new_v2_final is not. A workable taxonomy has three properties:

  • A naming convention enforced at creation, with fixed positions for market, channel, funnel stage, objective and audience, separated by a fixed delimiter.
  • A mapping table for anything that does not follow the convention, owned by a named person and versioned.
  • A fallback bucket (“unmapped”) that is monitored. If unmapped spend exceeds a small share of total spend, channel-level numbers are not reliable that week.

Renaming or regrouping campaigns mid-period. If “Paid Social” is split into “Prospecting” and “Retargeting” in June, every year-on-year comparison at the sub-channel level is broken for twelve months unless history is remapped.

2.3 Tracked versus system-of-record outcomes

Tracked outcomes come from pixels, SDKs and tags: they are what the ad platforms and web analytics tools see. System-of-record outcomes come from your own backend: accounts actually opened, payments actually received. The two diverge for many reasons, most of which have nothing to do with marketing:

  • users decline consent or block trackers;
  • the same person converts on a different device or browser than the one they clicked on;
  • the platform models conversions it cannot observe and adds them to the count;
  • attribution windows and counting rules differ between tools;
  • tags break during site releases.

Use the system of record as the outcome for every incrementality question. Use tracked outcomes for what they are good at: fast, granular, relative signals inside a channel. And monitor the ratio between the two, because a sudden change in that ratio is the most reliable early warning of a tracking problem.

2.4 Signal loss

Since around 2020, the amount of user-level data available to measure advertising has fallen sharply, and it is not coming back.

  • Mobile app tracking. Apple’s App Tracking Transparency (introduced with iOS 14.5) requires opt-in for cross-app tracking. Most users decline. App campaign measurement on iOS now runs largely through aggregated, delayed, privacy-thresholded postbacks.
  • Browsers. Safari and Firefox block third-party cookies by default and limit the lifetime of some first-party cookies. Chrome’s plans have shifted several times; do not build a measurement strategy that depends on any browser’s behavior staying fixed.
  • Consent regimes. In the EU and UK, GDPR and ePrivacy rules mean a meaningful share of users never consent to marketing tags. Consent rates vary by country, by device and by banner design, which means tracking coverage itself varies by market.
  • Modeled conversions. Platforms fill the gap with statistical models of the conversions they can no longer observe. The reported number is therefore partly a model output whose method you cannot inspect.

The practical consequences are consistent. Platform-reported conversions are less complete and less comparable over time than they used to be. User-level methods such as multi-touch attribution have lost much of their input. Aggregate methods, which only need spend and outcomes by date and market, are largely unaffected. That is a large part of why marketing mix models and geo experiments have come back into fashion.

2.5 The change log

Every analysis that compares two periods implicitly assumes nothing else changed between them. In practice something always did. A change log is a single table, maintained by the measurement team, recording every event that could break a comparison:

Date Market Type Description Affects
2025-03-04 All Tracking New consent banner design Tracked conversions, all web channels
2025-04-15 DE Promo Spring bonus offer launched All outcomes in DE
2025-05-02 All Naming Paid social split into prospecting and retargeting Sub-channel reporting
2025-06-10 FR Outage Sign-up form errors for 6 hours Sign-ups in FR

The log costs a few minutes a week and prevents the most common analytical error in marketing: attributing a change in a metric to a campaign when it was really caused by a tracking or product change in the same window.

2.6 Worked example: a campaign that seems to hurt

We simulate a year of daily data for one market. The system of record (backend sign-ups) has a weekly pattern, mild growth and noise. On day 200 a new campaign launches that truly lifts sign-ups by 4%. Ten days earlier, on day 190, a consent banner change silently reduces the share of sign-ups the tracking tools can see by 12%.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
days = np.arange(365)
weekly = 1 + 0.15 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * days / 7)
trend = 1 + 0.0004 * days
campaign = np.where(days >= 200, 1.04, 1.0)            # true +4% lift from day 200
backend = rng.poisson(400 * weekly * trend * campaign)  # system of record

coverage = np.where(days >= 190, 0.62, 0.70)            # tracking coverage drops on day 190
tracked = rng.binomial(backend, coverage)

df = pd.DataFrame({"day": days, "backend": backend, "tracked": tracked})
pre, post = df.query("170 <= day < 200"), df.query("200 <= day < 230")
for col in ["tracked", "backend"]:
    change = post[col].mean() / pre[col].mean() - 1
    print(f"{col:8s}: 30 days after vs 30 days before launch = {change:+.1%}")
## tracked : 30 days after vs 30 days before launch = -4.1%
## backend : 30 days after vs 30 days before launch = +3.4%

Read on tracked conversions, a naive before-and-after comparison says the campaign coincided with a drop. Read on the backend, it says the opposite. Neither 30-day comparison is a valid estimate of the campaign effect (Chapter 3 explains why), but the tracked version is not even measuring the right thing.

The tracking break is easy to find if you look at the ratio of tracked to backend outcomes. Below, a simple least-squares search finds the single day that best splits the ratio series into two segments with different means.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

ratio = (df["tracked"] / df["backend"]).to_numpy()

def best_split(x, min_seg=14):
    """Single change point minimizing within-segment squared error."""
    costs = [((x[:k] - x[:k].mean())**2).sum() + ((x[k:] - x[k:].mean())**2).sum()
             for k in range(min_seg, len(x) - min_seg)]
    k = int(np.argmin(costs)) + min_seg
    return k, x[:k].mean(), x[k:].mean()

k, before, after = best_split(ratio)
print(f"Break detected at day {k}: ratio {before:.3f} -> {after:.3f}")
## Break detected at day 190: ratio 0.698 -> 0.620
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.2))
ax.plot(days, ratio, lw=0.8, color="#6B6B6B", label="Daily tracked / backend")
ax.hlines([before, after], [0, k], [k, 364], color="#1F4E79", lw=2, label="Segment means")
ax.axvline(k, ls="--", color="#C8102E", lw=1.2, label="Detected break")
ax.axvline(200, ls=":", color="#9A5B00", lw=1.5, label="Campaign launch")
ax.set_xlabel("Day"); ax.set_ylabel("Ratio")
ax.legend(loc="lower left", fontsize=8, ncol=2)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Figure 2.1: Tracked-to-backend ratio. The detected break (dashed) lands on the consent change, ten days before the campaign launch (dotted).

In real work, replace the toy search with a proper change-point library (the ruptures package in Python, changepoint in R) and run it as a scheduled check on the ratio for every market and channel. A break in the ratio does not tell you what happened, only where to look; the change log usually tells you the rest.

2.7 Checks before any analysis

Before fitting anything, run a short battery of checks on the panel. Each one corresponds to a failure that has burned real projects.

Check What you are looking for Typical cause
Completeness Missing days, markets or channels; zero spend that should not be zero Failed extracts, API limits, new accounts not connected
Duplicates The same spend counted twice Overlapping extracts, agency and platform reporting the same campaign
Currency and time zone Spend jumps at month boundaries, conversions shifted by a day Mixed currencies, platform time zone differs from warehouse
Unmapped share Spend not assigned to any channel New campaigns that break the naming convention
Ratio stability Breaks in tracked / backend, clicks / impressions, spend / impressions Tracking changes, auction or pricing changes
Outliers Single days far from the series Outages, bot traffic, data errors, real but one-off events
Variation Channels whose spend barely moves Always-on channels; a model cannot learn their effect from history

The last check matters more than it looks. A model can only learn the effect of a channel from variation in its spend. A channel that ran at the same level every week for two years has no variation to learn from, and no amount of modeling will fix that. The fix is to create variation on purpose, which is one of the strongest arguments for running experiments (Part II).

2.8 What to request for a model or a test

When you start an MMM or plan a geo test, the data request is the first deliverable. A good request specifies:

  • History. At least two years of weekly data for an MMM, three if you want to separate seasonality from trend with confidence. For a geo test, at least as many pre-period weeks as you plan to test, and preferably several times more.
  • Grain. Weekly or daily, by the smallest geography at which media can be bought separately.
  • Media. Spend and a delivery metric (impressions or GRPs) per channel, per geography, per period, including channels that are hard to collect such as TV, out-of-home, podcasts and sponsorships.
  • Outcomes. The business outcome from the system of record, plus any intermediate outcome you want to model (site visits, sign-ups, activations).
  • Controls. Prices, promotions, product changes, distribution changes, holidays, and any known external shocks.
  • Events. The change log for the full period.

Starting to model before the data request is complete. Missing channels do not disappear; their effect is absorbed by whichever included variable happens to be correlated with them, and the model gives that variable the credit.

2.9 Checklist

  • Is the outcome taken from the system of record, not from tracking?
  • Are tracked and backend outcomes stored side by side, with their ratio monitored?
  • Is there a change log, and does any entry overlap the analysis window?
  • Is unmapped spend below an agreed threshold for every week in the window?
  • Have currency, time zone and duplicate checks been run?
  • Does every channel you want to measure have enough spend variation to learn from?
  • Are all channels included, including offline ones, so that omitted spend does not bias the rest?
